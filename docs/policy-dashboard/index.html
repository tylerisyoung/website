    How stable is each rank?

    Why 1,000 simulations? The published evidence gives each policy a range, not one number (free trade: 90 to 2,800). Taking only the middle would hide how sure we are. So we re-run the whole ranking 1,000 times, each time picking every policy's ratio at random from within its published range, and record where it lands. The bar shows the ranks it landed on in the middle 90% of runs; the dot is its typical rank. A short bar means the rank is solid whatever the true number turns out to be; a long bar (like the innovation prizes, whose sources run from 8.9 to 348) means the rank depends on which study is right.

    How often does each policy make the top 20?

    Out of the same 1,000 re-runs, the share in which the policy finished in the top 20. 100% means it made the top 20 in every run, whichever end of its evidence range was drawn: that is a priority you can defend even if some of the studies are too optimistic. A policy near 50% is a coin flip that depends on which studies are right.

    Methodology

    The project

    This dashboard is the working engine behind two pieces of writing. The first is a book, Lighthearted Humans: Prosperity Without Pretense, which argues that the fastest way to make people richer, healthier and freer is to rank what actually works, fund the best buys, and get government out of the way of everything else. It uses the Copenhagen Consensus idea of comparing policies dollar for dollar, and asks what happens when free trade, sound money, property rights and deregulation are put on the same scale as bed nets and vaccines.

    The second is an academic paper (the thesis) that does the ranking formally. It is a comparative benefit-cost analysis built on published estimates: every benefit-cost ratio comes from other researchers' studies and official reports, put on one scale and ranked. Adding income, feasibility, evidence and cost criteria makes it a multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA). Everything is built in R.

    Methodology of the whole analysis

    Why this research matters: prioritization

    Every government, charity and aid agency has a limited budget, and every dollar spent on one policy is a dollar not spent on another. The world's development aid budget was $174 billion in 2025, and the UN's own list of Sustainable Development Goals has 169 targets, so trying to do everything means doing almost nothing well. Some policies return hundreds or thousands of dollars of benefit for every dollar spent; others destroy value. Ranking them on one honest scale shows where the next dollar, or the next repealed rule, does the most good. The question is not “is this a good cause?” but “what should come first?”

    How this analysis was conducted

    Forty policies were chosen in four blocks of ten. Climate: five proposals that rank highest in international climate surveys (the UN Peoples' Climate Vote, Dechezleprêtre et al., the European Investment Bank) and five official proposals (the Green New Deal, the Paris-strength carbon price, the C40 consumption targets, combustion-engine bans and privately funded innovation prizes). Popular: ten policies with majority support in public-opinion surveys. Health: ten programmes rated best buys by the Copenhagen Consensus, the Disease Control Priorities project, the WHO and GiveWell, all scored as government or donor spending. Freedom: two reforms from each of the five areas of the Fraser Institute's Economic Freedom of the World index. For each policy, published studies were collected: peer-reviewed research first, then official estimates, think-tank studies and books. Each policy was given a benefit-cost ratio range from those sources (or built from published numbers where no ratio existed, with the arithmetic shown), a full yearly cost, an evidence grade on a nine-level rubric, and sourced ratings for who benefits and how hard it is to implement. Studies that measured a narrower version of a policy were set aside but listed as counter-evidence. The ranges were then run through 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations to see how stable each rank is, and scored in R, with the benefit-cost ratio always counting in full and optional dials to tilt toward other criteria. The result is the published ranking, which any reader can stress-test with the controls on the left.

    Every item on this dashboard, explained

    Benefit-cost ratio (BCR)
    Dollars of benefit to society for every dollar of cost, in today's money. Above 1, a policy creates more than it costs; below 0, its harms exceed its benefits. Each policy has a published range, taken from the sources or built from published numbers with the arithmetic shown in the Policy explorer.
    Adjusted ratio
    The published ratio after the evidence settings are applied. The dot on each chart is its middle result across 1,000 simulations; the line covers the middle 90%.
    Evidence grade (A+ to C−)
    Each policy gets the grade of its best supporting evidence, using the nine-level rubric on the Uncertainty tab: from several peer-reviewed controlled studies (A+) down to published theory built by analogy (C−).
    Evidence skepticism (slider)
    How far to pull weaker-graded ratios toward break-even (1:1) in log space. At the left (“All data”) every published number counts at face value; at the right (“Best data”) a C− ratio keeps 60% of its size in log terms and an A+ ratio keeps all of it. The middle is the published setting.
    Feasibility tilt
    The benefit-cost ratio always counts in full; the ranking is not left to personal weights. The dial adds a tilt toward easy or hard policies. At the middle it has no effect; at either end it can move a policy's score by up to ±0.15 on the 0–1 value scale, so it can reorder close calls but not overturn a large gap in value. There is no separate evidence dial, because the skepticism slider already does that job.
    Who benefits
    Each policy is rated from its sources as benefiting mostly the poor, everyone, or mostly higher incomes. The rating uses the distribution evidence in the sources (for example, trade gains concentrated in low-income countries, or energy mandates that raise costs most for poor households).
    Difficulty to implement (easy / moderate / hard)
    Rated from sourced facts with one rule for all 40 policies:
    Easy: one agency, regulator or funder can do it under existing law, and it has been done before (e.g., an agency rule, a philanthropic prize).
    Moderate: needs ordinary legislation in one country, or a sustained funding scale-up, with a working precedent somewhere (e.g., Hong Kong's zero tariffs for unilateral free trade).
    Hard: needs a constitutional amendment, a binding agreement among many countries, or the dismantling of a major institution with no modern precedent.
    The reason and its source are shown for each policy in the Policy explorer.
    Full cost per year
    Everything it takes to carry the policy out, in US dollars a year: public spending, administration and enforcement, the compliance spending it forces on households and firms, and output (GDP) lost. The highest credible published cost sits behind the low end of each range. Money moved from one group to another is a transfer worth $1 to the people who receive it; the deadweight loss of the taxes that fund it is a real cost (34 cents per $1 in the mainstream general-equilibrium estimate, about $2 per $1 at the low end).
    Rules that destroy value
    Rules whose main effect is lost consumer value or output (rationing caps, price controls, mandates and transfer programmes) are scored in net-damage form: the losses are subtracted from the benefits, and the ratio is measured per dollar actually spent or transferred to carry the rule out. A ratio below zero means the rule destroys more than every dollar put into it.
    Taxes beyond what can be raised
    At the peak of the Laffer curve the US can raise at most about 7.4% of GDP in extra tax revenue and the EU-14 about 3.1% (Trabandt & Uhlig 2012). Programmes whose new tax-financed spending is larger than that (the Green New Deal, a global basic income, Medicare for All) use the high published estimate of the tax burden, about $2 of deadweight per $1 raised (Feldstein 1999), at both ends of their range.
    Lost-income mortality
    Lower incomes cost lives: about one statistical death per $99.3 million of cost imposed (Broughel & Viscusi). Valued at the US government's $13.1 million value of a statistical life, each $1 of net income lost carries 13 cents of mortality cost, and each $1 gained saves the same. This step is applied to every policy after its range is drawn. Health programmes, whose benefits are already counted in lives, carry only the mortality cost of the taxes that fund them.
    Infant deaths in poor countries
    When world income falls, poor countries' incomes fall too. Each 1% fall in GDP per person raises infant mortality by 0.24 to 0.40 deaths per 1,000 births (Baird, Friedman & Schady 2011), on about 120 million births a year in low- and middle-income countries. Spillovers from Western economies use 0.6 to 1.4 points of developing-country growth per point of Western growth (World Bank). The Deaths tab lists these alongside deaths from lost income.
    Carbon values
    Avoided climate damage is valued at $36 per tonne of CO2 (Nordhaus) to $66 per tonne (DICE-2023, Barrage & Nordhaus 2024). Cuts made by only some countries are reduced for leakage to the rest of the world, which published estimates put at 12% to 130%.
    Monte Carlo simulation (1,000 runs)
    The sources give each policy a range, not one number. Each range is treated as a 90% interval of a lognormal distribution; the ranking is re-run 1,000 times with every policy's ratio drawn at random from its range. 1,000 runs is enough for the percentages to be stable to about ±2 points.
    Rank range and chance of making the top 20
    Across the 1,000 runs, the rank range shows where each policy landed in the middle 90% of runs, and the top-20 chance is the share of runs in which it finished 20th or better. A policy near 100% is a priority whichever of its studies is right.
    Published ranking and RESET
    The published ranking is the order at the default settings (skepticism and feasibility in the middle). RESET returns every control to those settings; the yellow box on the Ranking tab shows where the current top policy sits in that published ranking.
    Filters
    The policy-area filter hides policies from the charts and tables; ranks are still worked out across all 40.
    Development aid budget
    The headline on the Budget tab is official development aid from rich-country donors (OECD Development Assistance Committee; the UN uses the same figures): $174.3 billion in 2025. The other budgets are shown for scale, as money that could be re-allocated.
    Best buys vs. the 10 worst, and net value
    The best-buys side funds the highest-ratio policies above the slider threshold from the $174.3 billion aid budget, skipping any that no longer fit or that overlap a policy already funded. The counterfactual is shown two ways: the same amount the best buys actually spend split equally across the ten lowest-ratio policies that have a cost (value per dollar), and the ten lowest-ratio policies implemented in full at their sourced yearly cost, with overlapping policies counted once (the scale at which such mandates would actually run). Net value = today's value of all the future benefits the money buys (spending × ratio), minus the spending. Reforms that cost nothing, like free trade, add their gains on top for free, so they are not in the green total.
    Counter-evidence
    Studies that measure a narrower or different version of a policy, or use static models that leave out growth effects, are not used in the range. They are still listed under each policy in the Policy explorer with the reason, so every reader can see what was left out.

    Caveats

    Many of these policies have never been tried at full scale, so no study measures them directly; those rely on analogies and carry C grades. Ranges from different studies use different assumptions about discount rates, time horizons and regions. Treat the ranking as a transparent, adjustable argument about priorities, not a forecast.

    Methodology (paper version)

    The full methodology section of the accompanying paper, including formulas, the sensitivity scenarios and the limitations.

    3.1 Research design

    This study is a comparative benefit-cost analysis (BCA) built on published estimates. It does not produce new primary estimates of benefits and costs. Instead, it assembles benefit-cost ratios (BCRs) from existing studies and official reports, puts them on a common scale, and ranks the policies. This follows the approach of the Copenhagen Consensus projects (Lomborg, 2014, 2023), which also rank interventions by published or commissioned BCRs.

    The design departs from a single-criterion BCA in two ways. First, evidence quality, uncertainty, time preference and the mortality cost of lost income are modelled explicitly rather than taken at face value. Second, three further criteria (who benefits, feasibility and cost) are carried alongside the BCR, with evidence strength entering through the skepticism adjustment. Adding these criteria makes the study a multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA; Keeney & Raiffa, 1976; Belton & Stewart, 2002). The BCR remains the primary criterion throughout; the other criteria can shift a policy's score only within a bounded margin (Section 3.9).

    The analysis answers three questions:

    1. What order of priority do the published estimates imply?
    2. How robust is that order to the quality of the evidence, to the discount rate and to the reader's preferences?
    3. What would a fixed budget buy if allocated by that order, compared with allocating it to the lowest-ranked policies, and how many lives does each policy cost or save through its effect on incomes?

    Perspective and standing. The intended perspective is global: benefits and costs count wherever they fall. Each ratio inherits the standing of its sources. Most trade, health and development estimates are global or cover low- and middle-income countries, while many regulatory and tax estimates are for the United States or the European Union. Benefits are measured as each source measures them (welfare, consumer surplus, GDP, lives valued with a value of statistical life, or fiscal savings). Ratios are unit-free, so a common price year matters less than it would for net present values, but mixed standing and benefit concepts remain a limitation (Section 3.13).

    3.2 Policy selection

    Forty policies were selected in four blocks of ten, each by a stated rule:

    • Climate. Five measures that rank highest in international surveys of climate-policy preferences (the UNDP Peoples' Climate Vote, 2021; Dechezleprêtre et al., 2025; the European Investment Bank Climate Survey) and five official proposals: the US Green New Deal resolution (H.Res.109, 2019), a Paris-strength global carbon price, the C40 cities' consumption targets, combustion-engine sales bans and privately funded innovation prizes.
    • Popular. Ten policies with majority or near-majority support in public-opinion surveys (for example the Cooperative Election Study, Pew, YouGov and Ipsos). Popularity is the selection rule; results fall where the evidence puts them.
    • Health. Ten programmes rated best buys by the Copenhagen Consensus, the Disease Control Priorities project (DCP3), the WHO and GiveWell. All are scored as government or donor spending.
    • Freedom. Two reforms from each of the five areas of the Fraser Institute's Economic Freedom of the World index: size of government, legal system and property rights, sound money, freedom to trade internationally, and regulation.

    Overlap groups. Some policies share or contain one another's benefits: Medicare for All includes drug-price negotiation; the Green New Deal includes renewable subsidies and replaces combustion vehicles; rent control is a price control; a zero-private-car rule contains a combustion-car ban. In the budget exercise only one member of each overlap group is funded or counted.

    3.3 Evidence search and source selection

    For each policy, published studies were collected in order of evidential weight: peer-reviewed research first, then official estimates from governments and international bodies, then think-tank studies and books. Each source was checked against its own text or abstract, and its reported estimate is recorded verbatim or with the arithmetic used. Where a regulator or proposer appraises its own policy, that appraisal is treated as the optimistic bound. Studies that measure a narrower or different version of a policy, or that are superseded by later work from the same authors, are kept in a counter-evidence register with the reason they are not used in the range. The final evidence base contains 631 unique works.

    3.4 Construction of benefit-cost ratios

    Where a source reports a BCR for the policy, its range is used directly. Where none exists, the ratio is built from published components, and the arithmetic is shown in each policy's documentation. Five conventions apply to every policy.

    Full cost. Cost counts public outlays, administration and enforcement, private compliance spending and output (GDP) lost, using the highest credible published cost behind the low end of each range and avoiding double counting where a general-equilibrium estimate already contains compliance costs.

    Transfers. Money moved from one group to another is worth $1 to its recipients per $1 spent, so it appears in both the benefit and the cost of a transfer programme. The deadweight loss of the taxes that fund it is a real cost: 34 cents per $1 in the central general-equilibrium estimate (Ballard, Shoven & Whalley, 1985) and about $2 per $1 at the low end (Feldstein, 1999). Public-employment pay is worth about half the gross wage to participants, because they give up other income and time (Jalan & Ravallion, 2003).

    Net-damage form. Rules whose main effect is lost consumer value or output (rationing caps, price controls, mandates and transfer programmes) are scored as (benefits − losses) / spending, where spending is the money actually spent or transferred to carry the rule out. Lost consumer value, lost output and induced deaths are subtracted from benefits. A ratio below zero means the rule destroys more than every dollar put into it.

    Financing limits. At the peak of the Laffer curve the United States can raise at most about 7.4% of GDP in additional tax revenue and the EU-14 about 3.1% (Trabandt & Uhlig, 2012, Table 7). Near or past the peak the marginal burden of taxation rises without limit (Browning, 1987). Programmes whose new tax-financed outlays exceed 7.4% of GDP (the Green New Deal, a global basic income, Medicare for All) therefore use Feldstein's estimate of about $2 of deadweight per $1 at both ends of their range.

    Carbon values and leakage. Avoided CO2 is valued at $36 per tonne (Nordhaus, 2019) to $66 per tonne (DICE-2023; Barrage & Nordhaus, 2024). Cuts made by only part of the world are reduced for leakage to the rest, which published estimates put at a 12% mean (Böhringer, Balistreri & Rutherford, 2012) to 130% (Babiker, 2005).

    3.5 Evidence grading

    Each policy is graded on a nine-level scale according to the strongest evidence that supports its range. The scale follows the logic of the GRADE framework in health research (Guyatt et al., 2008): study design sets the starting level, and indirectness lowers it.

    3.6 Cost, distribution and feasibility ratings

    Cost. The cost criterion is the full yearly cost of Section 3.4, in US dollars. Three repeals are costed at zero because no source identifies an implementation cost.

    Who benefits. Each policy is rated as benefiting mostly the poor, everyone, or mostly higher incomes, from the distributional evidence in its sources.

    Feasibility. Feasibility is rated with a single rule applied to all 40 policies. Easy: one agency, regulator or funder can act under existing law, and a precedent exists. Moderate: ordinary legislation in one country, or a funding scale-up, with a working precedent somewhere. Hard: a constitutional amendment, a binding agreement among many countries, or the dismantling of a major institution with no modern precedent. The counts are 4 easy, 24 moderate and 12 hard.

    3.7 Adjustments for lost-income mortality, evidence quality and time

    Lost-income mortality. Lower incomes raise mortality. Broughel and Viscusi (2017) estimate about one statistical death for every $99.3 million of cost imposed (range $75.4–123.2 million). Valued at the US Department of Health and Human Services value of a statistical life, $13.1 million, each $1 of net income lost carries 13.1/99.3 = $0.132 of mortality cost, and each $1 gained saves the same. Each draw of a policy's ratio is adjusted as

    BCR′ = BCR + 0.132 × (a + b · BCR),

    where a + b·BCR is the net income change per $1 of cost: a = −1 and b = 1 when benefits are income or avoided damage, and a = −1, b = 0 for health programmes, whose benefits are already counted in lives. The step is monotone, so it preserves each policy's ordering of draws.

    Infant deaths in poor countries. A 1% fall in GDP per person raises infant mortality by 0.24 to 0.40 deaths per 1,000 births (Baird, Friedman & Schady, 2011; 1.7 million births in 59 countries), on about 119.9 million births a year in low- and middle-income countries (World Bank). Losses in Western economies spread to developing economies at 0.6 (United States; Kose et al., 2017) to about 1.4 (G7; Huidrom, Kose & Ohnsorge, 2017) points of growth per point. Infant deaths enter the ratios of the policies with a global or Western GDP loss, valued at $198,000 each, the newborn value implied by the Copenhagen Consensus maternal and newborn estimate.

    Evidence-quality adjustment. Estimates from weaker designs tend to overstate effects. Every BCR is therefore shrunk toward break-even in proportion to its grade: BCRk for positive ratios and k·BCR for zero or negative ratios, with k = 1 − s(1 − wg). The grade weight wg runs from 1.00 for A+ down in steps of 0.05 to 0.60 for C−. The skepticism parameter s ∈ [0, 1] is set by the reader, with a default of 0.5. The adjustment is a transparent heuristic in the spirit of Bayesian shrinkage toward a skeptical prior (Gelman et al., 2013).

    Discounting. Published ratios are already discounted, mostly at 3–8%. To test other rates, benefits are assumed to arrive on average ten years after costs, and each ratio is multiplied by ((1 + 0.05)/(1 + r))10. Because the same factor multiplies every ratio, the discount rate does not reorder policies; it changes which policies clear the budget threshold.

    3.8 Uncertainty analysis

    Each published range is treated as the 90% interval of a lognormal distribution (Morgan & Henrion, 1990): log-mean (ln L + ln H)/2 and log-standard deviation (ln H − ln L)/(2 × 1.645), with a floor of 0.10. Ranges that touch or cross zero use a normal distribution with the same 90% interval (floor 0.05). The analysis draws N = 1,000 values per policy (random seed 42), applies the adjustments of Section 3.7 to each draw, and recomputes the full ranking for every draw. This yields, for each policy, the median adjusted BCR and its 5th–95th percentile interval, a 90% rank interval, and the probability of ranking in the top 20. With 1,000 draws the Monte Carlo standard error of any reported probability is at most 1.6 percentage points. Draws are independent across policies.

    3.9 Multi-criteria aggregation

    All criteria are rescaled to [0, 1]. Value is the signed log of the median adjusted BCR, sign(x) log10(1 + |x|), min-max rescaled. Who benefits scores 1 for mostly the poor, 0.5 for everyone and 0 for mostly higher incomes. Feasibility scores 1 for easy, 0.5 for moderate and 0 for hard. Cost scores 1 minus the rescaled log10(1 + annual cost). The score of policy i is

    Si = Vi + τ Σk tk (cik − 0.5),   τ = 0.3,

    where tk ∈ [−1, 1] is the reader's tilt on criterion k, with a default of 0. One tilt can move a score by at most ±0.15 on the 0–1 value scale. The published ranking is the order of Si at the defaults (s = 0.5, r = 5%, all tilts 0), where it equals the order of median adjusted BCRs.

    3.11 Deaths and total damage

    For each policy, deaths a year from lost income are its income lost (administration, compliance, output lost and non-transfer public spending) divided by $99.3 million. Infant deaths in poor countries follow Section 3.7. The two overlap slightly and are reported side by side, not added. Twenty-year totals multiply the yearly figures by 20. Policies that raise income save lives at the same rate. Annual net value is the policy's cost base multiplied by (median adjusted BCR − 1).

    3.12 Budget exercise

    The headline budget is official development assistance from Development Assistance Committee donors: USD 174.3 billion in 2025 (OECD, 2026). Best buys. Policies above a reader-chosen ratio threshold (default 1) are funded in descending order of median adjusted BCR, skipping a policy whose cost no longer fits or that overlaps one already funded; at the default threshold 18 policies are funded for USD 144.5 billion. Counterfactual. The same amount is split equally across the costed policies among the ten with the lowest ratios. Full implementation. The ten best and the ten worst are also carried out in full at their sourced yearly cost, with overlapping policies counted once; free reforms use their sourced yearly gain. Net value is spending × (ratio − 1) on each policy's cost base.

    3.13 Software, reproducibility and limitations

    The analysis is written in base R and runs in the browser through Shinylive. Every policy's range, arithmetic, cost basis, grade reason and sources are published with the dashboard, and an Excel workbook reproduces every calculation and ranking with the same 1,000 simulation draws.

    Limitations. Many of these policies have never been tried at full scale, so their ratios are built from components and analogies and carry lower grades. Ranges from different studies use different discount rates, horizons, regions and benefit concepts. Ratios are sensitive to whether a loss is counted as a cost or subtracted from benefits; the conventions of Section 3.4 are applied uniformly to limit this. The lost-income mortality rate is a US estimate applied to all policies, and the infant-mortality link is estimated from income shocks in developing countries. Draws are independent across policies. The ranking is a transparent, adjustable argument about priorities, not a forecast.
